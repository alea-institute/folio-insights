Chapter IV · § 4.2

Before the first question

A trial begins long before a witness takes the stand. A careful advocate prepares a motion in limine to identify a disputed use of proof before the jury encounters it. The request should describe the precise evidence, its proposed purpose, and the boundary the advocate asks the court to draw. A broad wish to exclude “anything prejudicial” gives the court little to decide.

Consider a witness who repeats a supplier’s assurance that a machine was safe. A hearsay objection cannot be assessed from the quoted words alone. Ask what proposition the speaker offers them to establish. The same sentence may be offered to prove the machine’s condition or to explain why its buyer stopped investigating.

The advocate should separate the purpose of the statement from the credibility of its speaker. If the purpose changes, the objection may change with it. When the court admits the words for a bounded purpose, request a limiting instruction that tells the jury how the statement may be used. An instruction that merely repeats the word “limited” has not explained the limit.

Preparation also includes preserving the objection: identify the ground, obtain a ruling, and, when necessary, make an offer of proof. The smaller proffer span sits within the broader preservation step; the two tags describe different claims about the same passage.

A bench conference may help counsel state the issue without repeating disputed material before the jury. Bring a written exhibit list so that the discussion refers to an identifiable item rather than to “that document from yesterday.”

Finally, the advocate must exercise judgment about which disputes deserve the court’s time. Here “judgment” names a capacity of counsel, not a judicial officer. A confident reading of the chapter should allow an uncertain machine label to remain uncertain.
